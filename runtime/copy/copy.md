## Tone, punctuation and length

- Write like a builder sending a useful note to another builder. Plain words, lowercase prose, natural contractions and direct statements. Preserve exact case in URLs, handles, addresses, code and product identifiers.
- Prefer a short introduction and one concise call CTA containing the link for initial emails and DMs. Aim for 20–40 body words in email and 15–30 in a DM; existing hard limits remain 60 and 40. Email subject: 1–6 plain descriptive words, no clickbait or fake reply prefix.
- Use ordinary periods and commas. A fragment is fine when clear. Avoid decorative dashes, ellipses, repeated punctuation, forced emoji, exclamation-heavy enthusiasm and fake typos. Lowercase alone does not make a message natural.
- One useful offer, one link, one honest next step: the supplied /call destination is for a call, not direct search access. Use a brief invitation to a call as the single CTA for initial emails and DMs. Do not add a second reply request or another engagement question.
- Character preferences remain uncalibrated until examples arrive. Count actual body/subject characters separately, including spaces and punctuation; do not invent sample-derived character bands. Keep a long required URL intact and shorten the prose around it.

## Specificity without a research recital

Use one recent, verified fact only when it explains why this offer fits this developer's work. Explain that fit in everyday language; omit the biography, homepage summary and compliments. Do not claim to have used, followed, loved or tested their product unless that is true and recorded. Do not presume a pain point, technical stack or buying intent. If the fit cannot be supported, hold the introduction rather than invent a personalized line.

Use accurate technical nouns where useful, but no feature inventory. Name the capability and what the recipient can actually do with it. Only use approved claims from the brief. Never convert 'we built something useful' into 'this is exactly what you need.'

## Channel and sequence judgment

An initial email or DM must make the sender, product and offer understandable without prior messages. Place the exact verified call destination in the body CTA, before the email signature when present. Later cold touches can stand alone without repeating the whole introduction or pretending there was a reply. Each should contribute a distinct supported detail; if there is nothing useful to add, hold rather than manufacture another angle.

A public reply must first address the actual post. A brief disclosed introduction and one final link are appropriate only when the product directly answers the post's subject or request. Do not paste the email introduction under an unrelated post. Aim for 3–12 words for a simple reaction; a relevant product introduction may use up to 30 words as an editorial limit. Respect the channel adapter's capabilities: a link-capable Markdown draft does not mean the existing public-comment sender accepts links.

Private conversation responses answer the actual incoming message. Do not restart the pitch, reintroduce ourselves or append a link automatically. A/B alternatives, when requested, serve the same real context; choose one, never send both.

## Five-point review required for every draft

Keep a concise review comment beside each individual draft, separate from outbound prose. Address these five checks with pass, revise or hold and a specific evidence-based explanation; a score cannot compensate for a failed factual or relevance check. Show copy and comments in chat for Jason’s review unless he requests a file. Reapply the checks to every channel version and substantive revision.

1. **One person.** Address the named recipient directly. Verify what this person authored or developed. Do not combine co-founders, borrow a co-founder's achievements or needs, or treat a company repost as the recipient's personal statement. Team-level facts remain team-level; do not recast them as individual work.
2. **Grounded context.** Complete the three context checks below. Cite the exact source and distinguish publication date from capture date. Disclose saved captures, inaccessible originals, stale facts and missing evidence. Three details from one post are one source, not three independent confirmations; do not force three facts into the message.
3. **A supported reason for this offer.** Explain the specific connection between the recipient's documented work or request and an approved Darwin capability. “They build agents” is insufficient. Separate stated needs from possible uses. Conditional phrasing such as “if you're exploring” does not repair an unsupported fit. Hold the pitch when the connection cannot be supported.
4. **A deliberate copy choice.** Explain which one detail earns space in this message, why it is more relevant than the other available details, and what the wording does for the recipient. State why the proposed channel/form is suitable or provisional. Public replies address the actual post; private responses answer the incoming message. Do not invent motivations to justify a draft after writing it.
5. **Clear, casual and accurate.** Check short lowercase peer language, natural punctuation, no generic praise or interchangeable filler, approved sender/offer claims, one honest next step and the requested signature/link where applicable. Measure body words and full-message characters. The /call link is a call destination, not direct access to search. Provide the final disposition and concrete rewrite or missing evidence; unsupported context means hold, not a cosmetic rewrite.

### Three context checks behind each copy choice

1. **What was actually said or built?** State the relevant fact, who said or built it, exact source, publication date when known and retrieval date. Separate direct evidence from a directory summary or secondhand attribution.
2. **What does that support—and leave unknown?** State the narrow conclusion supported about this person's development work. Explicitly list the missing need, workflow, integration or capability information that matters to the proposed offer. Keep hypotheses labeled; shared vocabulary does not establish relevance.
3. **Why this detail and this wording?** Give a concise editorial rationale connecting the chosen fact to a supported offer or a directly relevant non-promotional response. Explain why unused details were omitted. If there is no supported connection, record hold and the exact evidence needed; do not manufacture one from the other two checks.

Review comments summarize sources, assumptions and editorial decisions; they do not recite an internal step-by-step thought process. Save person-specific evidence and comments privately with the draft, never in the reusable voice guide.

## Reject and rewrite

- Generic praise: 'love what you’re building', 'impressive work', 'exciting journey'.
- Sales padding: 'unlock', 'supercharge', 'game-changing', 'seamless solution', 'take it to the next level', 'leverage our platform'.
- Outreach filler: 'hope you’re well', 'reaching out', 'just checking in', 'bumping this', 'quick question' with no real question.
- Fake familiarity, unsupported need/results, generic interview questions, feature triads, unnecessary greetings/sign-offs, multiple CTAs or invented urgency.
- A message that does not clearly say who is writing, what we built and what can be tried; an unsupported link; a public reply unrelated to its post.

These are concrete editorial checks, not an AI detector or a guarantee of conversion. Review meaning as well as words: swapping 'supercharge' for another sales verb does not fix a generic pitch. The final test is whether a busy developer can understand the useful offer on one read without decoding marketing language.
